A minimal bonded discrete element model for sea ice

Resolving fracture and floe–floe interaction directly, with a small enough parameter space to actually calibrate.

Sea ice is a critical component of the Earth system. It mediates ocean heat uptake, drives the global overturning circulation, buffers vulnerable ice shelves from ocean waves, and serves as habitat for organisms from the primary producers at the base of the food web up through keystone species like the polar bear. Through polar amplification, climate change is affecting the polar regions more than anywhere else on Earth.

Despite the urgent need for accurate predictions of the future sea ice state, current climate models struggle to reproduce sea ice trends on seasonal and decadal timescales. These models simulate sea ice as a continuous medium, despite the discontinuities that arise from fracturing and floe–floe interaction. Discrete element models (DEMs) resolve those discontinuities directly, which makes them valuable tools for developing parameterizations of the subgrid-scale physics that set the sea ice momentum budget in climate models.

Because DEMs resolve interactions between individual elements, they are typically expensive to run and carry many unconstrained parameters. In this work we develop, calibrate, and evaluate a minimal bonded discrete element model for process-based studies — trading some physics for a small parameter space and real computational efficiency.
