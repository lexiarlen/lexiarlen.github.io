Modeling sea ice dynamics across scales

I am a PhD candidate in Earth System Science at Stanford University, in the Polar Ocean Dynamics Group led by Prof. Earle Wilson. I am broadly interested in how small-scale sea ice dynamics can shape large-scale climate trends. To this end, I have developed a simple bonded discrete element model, which serves as a tool to help build intuition and parameterizations for floe-scale processes.

Research Interests

Sea ice is a critical component of the Earth system. It mediates ocean heat uptake, drives the global overturning circulation, buffers vulnerable ice shelves from ocean waves, and serves as habitat for organisms from the primary producers at the base of the food web up through keystone species like the polar bear. Through polar amplification, climate change is affecting the polar regions more than anywhere else on Earth.

Despite the urgent need for accurate predictions of the future sea ice state, current climate models struggle to reproduce sea ice trends on seasonal and decadal timescales. These models simulate sea ice as a continuous medium, despite the discontinuities that arise from fracturing and floe–floe interactions. Discrete element models (DEMs) resolve those discontinuities directly, which makes them valuable tools for developing parameterizations of these discrete processes for climate models.

Because DEMs resolve interactions between individual elements, they are typically expensive to run and have many unconstrained parameters. In my PhD I developed, calibrated, and evaluated a minimal bonded discrete element model where I traded some physics for a small parameter space and computational efficiency. In ongoing work, I am using this model to characterize the emergent sea ice floe size distribution with the aim of improving parameterizatins of floe-scale processes in global climate models.
